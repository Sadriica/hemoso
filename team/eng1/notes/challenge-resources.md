## 1. Five hazards (https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazards/)
| Hazard | What the page says | Link |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Space radiation | Raises cancer risk, can harm the central nervous system, thinking, movement and behaviour; NASA monitors radiation and crew exposure continuously. No doses given | https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazard-space-radiation/ |
| Isolation and confinement | Behavioural responses; sleep loss, circadian desynchronization and work overload add up; journaling used to assess mental state; light therapy as countermeasure | https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazard-isolation-and-confinement/ |
| Distance from Earth | Mars about 140 million miles away on average, trip about 3 years; communication delay up to 20 min one way; crews handle medical emergencies with minimal support | https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazard-distance-from-earth/ |
| Gravity fields | Weight-bearing bones lose about 1 % to 1.5 % mineral density per month; fluid shift toward the head (vision); kidney stone risk; orientation, balance, motion sickness, fainting at gravity transitions | https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazard-gravity-fields/ |
| Hostile closed environments | Temperature, pressure, lighting, noise, microbes; stress hormones can rise and immunity change. No values given | https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazard-hostile-closed-environments/ |

## 2. Risks of human spaceflight (https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/risks/), 17 in three groups
Each risk page links its formal description and evidence on the Human Research
Roadmap (https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/evidence/).

| Risk | Monitoring-relevant content on the page | Evidence link on the page |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cardiovascular adaptations | Heart imaged by ultrasound; concerns: low blood pressure and fainting at landing, arrhythmias, clots. Countermeasures: exercise, diet, lower-body compression, fluids before landing. No thresholds | https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/evidence/reports/Cardio%20Risk%20EB%202022%20FINAL_4-3-2023.pdf |
| Altered immune responses | Blood, saliva, urine samples; reported symptoms: cold sores, rashes, atypical allergies, infections; viral reactivation. No thresholds | https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Risks/risk.aspx?i=85 |
| Inadequate sleep and irregular schedules | About 16 sunrises a day on ISS; LED lighting and scheduled rest as countermeasures. No sleep-hour figures on the page | https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Risks/risk.aspx?i=100 |
| Behavioural conditions and psychiatric disorders | Anxiety, depression; affects sleep, morale, decisions. Countermeasures: journaling, plants, windows, privacy, contact with family and clinicians. No tools or thresholds | https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Risks/risk.aspx?i=99 |
| Bone changes | Density loss 1 % to 1.5 % per month on 4 to 6 month missions; scans before and after flight; height up to about 3 %. Countermeasures: resistive exercise, bisphosphonates | https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Risks/risk.aspx?i=77 |
| Cardiorespiratory and musculoskeletal fitness | Average loss 10 % to 12 % of pre-mission strength and aerobic fitness; 12 % to 38 % of crew lose more than 20 %; exercise 1.5 h a day, 6 to 7 days a week; periodic fitness self-evaluations | https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Risks/risk.aspx?i=180 |
| Food and nutrition | Menu fatigue; more than 200 foods on ISS; no individual measures on the page | https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Evidence/other/Nutrition%20ER_human_adaptation_2020_final.pdf |
| SANS (eye and brain) | About 70 % of ISS astronauts show some swelling at the back of the eye; fundoscope imaging; risk rises with mission length | https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Risks/risk.aspx?i=105 |
| Sensorimotor and vestibular | Most astronauts get motion sickness; motion sickness on Earth does not predict it in space; medication plus training | https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Risks/risk.aspx?i=88 |
| Others, not opened yet | Decompression sickness, host-microorganism interactions, hypoxia, EVA injury, inadequate teamwork, systems operations support, dynamic loads, radiation-induced cancers | https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/risks/ |

Common to every risk page: **no clinical thresholds**. Any "when it needs
attention" value must come from another source (evidence report, NASA-STD-3001
or clinical literature), or be marked Assumption.

## 5. What this means for the five questions
- **Measurable in space, and how:** the challenge's example indicators all have
  an on-board method in these pages: blood pressure, heart rate, temperature and
  oxygen saturation (Bio-Monitor, Tempus Pro, T-Mini), sleep (actigraphy and the
  daily questionnaire), mood (daily questionnaire, journaling). Blood sugar is
  not covered by any page read; it needs its own source.
- **Hazard effects:** each risk page ties a body system to the five hazards.
- **When it needs attention:** not on any page read. This is the main gap.
- **Prevention:** each risk page lists countermeasures (exercise, lighting,
  fluids, compression, diet).

## 7. Level 3: the four evidence PDFs (read Sat 14 Nov, 10:30)
Page numbers are PDF pages. Numbers are quoted from the documents; check the
page before citing.

### Cardiovascular evidence report (2022, 161 pages)
https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/evidence/reports/Cardio%20Risk%20EB%202022%20FINAL_4-3-2023.pdf
- In flight, the primary concerns are dysrhythmias and venous thromboembolism;
  whether spaceflight causes or increases dysrhythmias is "inconclusive" (p8).
- In-flight heart rate and systolic and diastolic blood pressure were lower than
  preflight; they rose past baseline at re-entry (p23, Holter and BP recordings).
- One crew member had a non-sustained 14-beat ventricular tachycardia, maximum
  215 beats per minute (p13). One third of astronauts who flew before 1996 had
  premature atrial or ventricular complexes during EVA (p13).
- 13 astronauts on 6-month missions: no significant increase in premature atrial
  contractions; no atrial fibrillation (p14).
- A jugular vein thrombus was found by ultrasound in an ISS crew member; NASA now
  runs targeted ultrasound surveillance (p8, p20).
- Orthostatic intolerance after landing: 4 of 6 (66 %) ISS astronauts could not
  finish a 10-minute 80° tilt test on landing day, versus 13 of 66 (20 %) Shuttle
  astronauts; most recover within 1 to 3 days (p22 to p23). More than 80 % of
  female crew became presyncopal on a postflight tilt test versus about 20 % of
  men (p23).
- Countermeasures: in-flight exercise (does not prevent presyncope), fluid and
  salt loading, compression garments (for example 55 mmHg at the ankle), thigh
  cuffs (pp33 to 35, p87).
- Long-term: lifetime cardiovascular risk rises above 0.5 Gy-Eq of radiation (p8).
- Thresholds for a self-monitoring app: none. The report gives no heart-rate or
  blood-pressure cut-offs for in-flight attention.

### Fracture evidence report (34 pages)
https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Evidence/reports/Fracture.pdf
- Bone mineral density falls 1 % to 1.5 % per month at weight-bearing sites (hip,
  lumbar spine, legs), measured by DXA before and after 4 to 6 month missions (p4).
- No data validate a percent loss in BMD as a predictor of fracture (p4).
- Monthly losses exceed yearly losses in elderly people (p12).

### OCHMO technical brief, bone loss (OCHMO-TB-030)
https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/ochmo-tb-030-bone-loss.pdf
- "No system to date measures bone density in-flight" (p20): bone cannot be a
  self-measured in-flight indicator, only a risk explained with its countermeasures.
- NASA-STD-3001 Vol 1 [V1 4027]: countermeasures shall keep hip and spine BMD at
  or above 95 % of pre-mission values, femoral neck at or above 90 % (pp20, 24 to 25).
- T-score bands: preflight certification -1.0 or greater; post-flight -2.0 or
  greater (p8). FRAX above 3 % hip or 20 % major fracture leads to referral (p9).
- Vitamin D: optimal 25-OH vitamin D 35 to 90 ng/ml; maintenance 1,000 IU daily
  or 5,000 IU weekly (p17).
- Exercise: ARED up to 600 pound-force; 6 to 7 sessions a week; earlier
  requirement 2 hours per workday (pp13, 15).

### Nutrition evidence report (2020, 135 pages)
https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/Evidence/other/Nutrition%20ER_human_adaptation_2020_final.pdf
- Body mass losses of 1 % to 5 % of preflight mass have been typical; more than
  10 % on Mir; average loss 2.4 % of body weight per 100 days (p22). Body mass is
  measured in flight (Figure 9).
- Lower energy intake goes with more weight loss (Apollo, r = -0.71, p22).
- Standard menu about 2300 to 2400 kcal per day (p12).
- Requirements table (p11): fluid 1 to 1.5 ml/kcal and more than 2000 ml per day;
  vitamin D 25 µg (1000 IU); carbohydrate 50 % to 55 % of energy.
- Blood glucose: carbohydrate requirements in flight are "thought to be similar
  to those on Earth"; few studies, with conflicting results (p23). No in-flight
  glucose thresholds.

## 8. NASA-STD-3001 Volume 1, Revision C (1 Nov 2023, current)
https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/nasa-std-3001-vol-1-rev-c-with-signature.pdf
(Revision B, 2022, was read first; the values below were checked in Revision C.)

What it requires that a monitoring tool can serve:
- [V1 3003] Programs shall provide resources "to monitor physiological and
  psychosocial well-being" in mission. Revision B lists what preventive care
  includes: periodic monitoring of general health status, nutrition intake, fitness,
  flexibility and balance, work/rest schedules and sleep, environment, hearing,
  psychosocial countermeasures, SANS, neck and leg veins, immune function.
- [V1 3004] In-mission medical care includes "assessing, recording, monitoring, and
  trending vital signs" (Revision C adds "additional physiological and behavioral
  health signs"), private transmission of medical data, and asynchronous behavioural
  health tools on electronic devices.

Numbers it sets (permissible outcome limits, mostly relative to the person's own baseline):
| Requirement | Limit | Can the crew track it in flight? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [V1 4003] In-mission aerobic capacity | At or above 80 % of pre-mission | Indirectly (exercise sessions, fitness self-evaluations) |
| [V1 4001] VO2max with microgravity EVAs | At or above 32.9 ml/min/kg | Measured by the medical team |
| [V1 4024] In-mission muscle strength | At or above 80 % of baseline | Indirectly (exercise loads) |
| [V1 4027] Bone mineral density | Hip and spine at or above 95 % of pre-mission; femoral neck 90 % | No: measured before and after flight |
| [V1 4020] Nutrition | 100 % of calculated nutrient and energy requirements (Rev B said no less than 90 %) | Yes: intake and body mass |
| [V1 4011] Behavioural health and cognition | "Within clinically accepted values as determined by behavioral health evaluations" | Self-report feeds those evaluations; no number given |
| [V1 4014] Work, exercise and sleep | Planned hours shall have established limits | Yes: sleep and work hours; limits set by the program, not this document |
| [V1 4030] Career radiation dose | Less than 600 mSv | No: dosimetry team |
Revision B Table 3 also lists 30-day and 1-year organ dose limits (for example
blood-forming organs 250 mGy-Eq in 30 days).

Sample Integrated Medical Model output (Rev C, Table E.2-1), expected events per
mission, top of the list: late insomnia 13.85, skin abrasion 9.86, skin rash 9.83,
eye abrasion 7.42, late headache 5.25, space motion sickness 4.37, diarrhoea 3.53,
nasal congestion 3.51, respiratory infection 3.46, back injury 3.41, back pain 3.15,
insomnia 2.70, CO2 headache 2.15, SANS 2.08. It is a sample output, not a mission
forecast; it shows which symptoms a self-report should cover.

What this settles:
- The standard asks for monitoring and trending of vital signs and well-being, which
  is what the challenge asks for; it can be cited as the reason the tool exists.
- Its limits are relative to each person's own baseline (80 %, 95 %), not absolute
  cut-offs. That supports comparing each astronaut with their own baseline.
- It does not set heart-rate, blood-pressure, temperature, sleep-hour or mood
  thresholds. Those still need clinical sources or the Assumption label.
